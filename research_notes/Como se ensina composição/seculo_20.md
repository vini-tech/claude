# How the great 20th-century composition teachers taught (c.1900–1990): methods, sequences, concrete exercises

Note on scope and sources: these notes are in English. All pitch-name examples marked "(constructed)" were written by the researcher to illustrate a rule cited from a source. They are not quotations from the books. Primary texts read directly: Schoenberg, *Fundamentos da Composição Musical* (the Brazilian translation of *Fundamentals of Musical Composition*, Edusp 1996); the opening of Hindemith's *Craft of Musical Composition*, Book 2; and Bomberger's article in the Journal of Music Theory Pedagogy on Boulanger and Rheinberger. Several key sources could not be reached because of paywalls or 403/503 errors: Messiaen-as-teacher, Stein's UCLA reminiscences, and the Cambridge "Schoenberg as teacher" chapter.

## 1. Arnold Schoenberg: progression, assignment types, correction, models

### Takeaway
Schoenberg taught a strict syntax-first progression: phrase → motive → connecting motive-forms → sentence and period → accompaniment → character → melody vs. theme → small ternary → irregular construction → minuet → scherzo → theme and variations → transitions/retransitions → rondo forms → sonata-allegro. Beethoven's piano sonatas were the near-exclusive model. His core drill was to generate many motive-forms from one basic motive with systematic variation operators, and then build real phrases from them. He corrected by asking for multiple solutions and then for the principle behind them.

### Cited Findings
- **Purpose of the book.** Stein's 1965 preface says *Fundamentals* was the last of three treatises from Schoenberg's US teaching. The other two are *Structural Functions of Harmony* (1954) and *Preliminary Exercises in Counterpoint* (1963). It was aimed at "average university students as well as talented students who aspire to become composers." It combines (1) analysis of masterworks, "with special emphasis on Beethoven's piano sonatas," and (2) composition practice in small and large forms. It extends the summary *Models for Beginners in Composition* (G. Schirmer, 1942) and deepens ch. XI of *Structural Functions* ("Progressions for Various Compositional Purposes"). Stein: the aim is to set out fundamental technical problems "and describe how to solve them in various ways," so the student develops critical judgment by practising many possibilities. — [Schoenberg, Fundamentos da Composição Musical (Edusp), pdf](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **Unmarked references.** In the book, every unattributed musical citation is Beethoven, and every untitled one is a piano sonata. — [Fundamentos pdf](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **Exact table of contents (the curricular sequence).**
  - Part I, Construction of Themes: 1 Concept of form; 2 The phrase; 3 The motive (subsections: use of the motive requires variation; how the motive is formed; treatment and use of the motive); 4 Connecting motive-forms (subsection: building phrases); 5 Construction of simple themes (beginning of the sentence; the "dominant form" / complementary repetition; antecedent of the period; consequent; completion of the sentence); 6 The accompaniment (accompaniment motive, types, voice leading, treatment of the bass); 7 Character and expression; 8 Melody and theme (vocal vs. instrumental melody); 9 Advice for self-criticism.
  - Part II, Small Forms: 1 Small ternary form (contrasting section, "upbeat chord," recapitulation); 2 Unequal, irregular and asymmetrical construction; 3 Minuet (with trio); 4 Scherzo (modulatory contrasting middle, "standard model," extensions, coda, trio); 5 Theme and variations (motive of the variation, counterpoint in variations, planning and organizing the set).
  - Part III, Large Forms: 1 Parts of large forms (transition, retransition, subordinate theme group, "lyric theme," coda); 2 Rondo forms (andante forms ABA and ABAB, simple rondos, large rondo ABA-C-ABA, sonata-rondo); 3 Sonata-allegro (exposition, transition, development/Durchführung, retransition, recapitulation, coda).
  - Source: [Fundamentos pdf, Sumário](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **Motive definition.** The constitutive factors of a motive are intervallic and rhythmic, combined into a contour that usually has an inherent harmony. Any feature "must be considered a motive if it is treated as such, i.e. if it is repeated, with or without variation." Variation = "a repetition in which some elements are changed and the rest preserved." Preserving rhythm produces coherence. Homophonic music is the style of "developing variation." Changes that are only local are "variants." — [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 3](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **The systematic variation catalogue (Exs. 17–29, all derived from a single arpeggiated chord).** This is effectively an operator list for a drill. Source: [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 3](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
  - **Rhythm:**
    1. Change note durations.
    2. Repeat some notes.
    3. Repeat certain rhythms.
    4. Shift rhythms to other beats.
    5. Add upbeats.
    6. Change the metre (rarely used within one piece).
  - **Intervals:**
    1. Change the order or direction of the notes.
    2. Add or omit intervals.
    3. Fill intervals with auxiliary notes.
    4. Shorten the motive by omission or condensation.
    5. Repeat certain patterns.
    6. Shift elements to other beats.
  - **Harmony:**
    1. Use inversions.
    2. Add new chords at the end.
    3. Insert intermediate chords.
    4. Substitute chords or use another succession.
  - **Melody:**
    1. Transpose to other degrees.
    2. Add passing harmonies.
    3. Treat the accompaniment "semi-contrapuntally."
  - Schoenberg's comment: such exploration "can be of great help in acquiring technical skill and developing a rich creative faculty."
- **Phrase-building from one motive (Exs. 30–34).** These examples show "various methods of producing a large number of different phrases from one basic motive." Some are suited to beginning a theme, others to continuation or contrast (especially those not starting on I).
  - Ex. 31 adds auxiliary notes but keeps every note of the motive.
  - Ex. 32 keeps the rhythm while changing intervals and direction. Combined with transposition to other degrees, this "is always used in traditional music to produce complete themes."
  - Ex. 33 combines rhythmic changes, auxiliary notes and interval/direction changes. Schoenberg: "even if some examples are rather stiff and exaggerated, one should never abandon the practice of doing exercises of this kind."
  - Ex. 34 uses rhythmic displacement and rearrangement to produce continuation and contrast material, with a warning that remote motive-forms endanger comprehensibility.
  - Requirement: the derivations must have "the character of real phrases, of complete musical units."
  - Source: [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 4](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **Harmonic frames for the sentence's opening ("tonic form" → "dominant form").** For practice, base the tonic form on I, I–V, I–V–I, I–IV or I–II, and answer it with a dominant-form repetition. In that repetition the rhythm and contour are kept, and contrast comes from changed harmony plus the necessary melodic adaptation. A precise, regular accompaniment is required even in short passages. — [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 5](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **Period practice.** "Only a small percentage of classical themes are periods… however, practising periods is a convenient way to become familiar with many technical problems." The period "differs from the sentence by postponing repetition." The antecedent can be built on progressions like Exs. 43–51. Even harmonic rhythm acts as a "primitive accompaniment motive." Building motive-form sketches by varying arpeggiated chords is "a valuable method" for keeping melody and harmony coordinated. — [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 5](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **Sentence standard model.** In the simplest case it has 8 bars, the first 4 being a phrase and its repetition. The continuation uses a kind of development comparable to "liquidation" (condensation), and many classical examples close to the standard model "will serve as practical exercise." — [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 5](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **Character assignment.** "Even with the smallest exercises the student should not work without a special character in mind. A poem, a story, a play or a film can serve as stimulus." Pieces should vary in tempo, rhythm and metre, to train the contrasts needed for large forms. — [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 7](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- **"Advice for self-criticism" checklist (ch. 9).** This is usable as an app feedback rubric. Source: [Fundamentos pdf, ch. 9](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
  1. Listen. Play or read melody and harmony separately; a good melody unaccompanied should be smooth, fluent, balanced.
  2. Analyse. Know which motive elements are developed; detect "empty segments."
  3. Eliminate superfluities: excess variation, ornamentation, too-remote motive-forms, abrupt register changes.
  4. Avoid monotony. Watch the highest note: the climax usually comes near the end. Keep a restricted range and do not move long in one direction.
  5. Watch the bass as a "second melody." It should be rhythmically balanced, varied in contour, with frequent inversions (especially of seventh chords).
  6. Make many sketches. "Change the method of variation frequently and train each method several times"; combine the best sketches. Motivic variation "is not a substitute for invention, but it can stimulate it, like an athlete's warm-up."
  7. Watch the harmony, the progressions and the bass line.
- **Models for Beginners.** The book bases motivic design, harmony and theme construction on the two-measure phrase. Root's OUP edition (2016) contains: Schoenberg's 1943 preface; the syllabus; music-example units starting with "Coordination of melody and harmony," then motives in two-measure phrases, then sentences and periods, with Minuet and Scherzo chapters; Appendix 2, Schoenberg's teaching schedule at UCLA; a "Primer in regions"; and Appendix 3, "Assignments from Schoenberg's beginning composition class" (Composition 105, UCLA). — [ECU catalog record](https://librarycatalog.ecu.edu/catalog/4808408); [Forsyths listing](https://www.forsyths.co.uk/music/books/199614-root-gordon-schoenberg-s-models-for-beginners-in-composition.html)
- **Developing variation in Models.** A University of Bern article notes that the concept of developing variation is absent from *Models for Beginners* and that its motivic examples are tonal. — [EJM / Bern (search snippet)](https://bop.unibe.ch/EJM/article/download/10953/14439/52682). Caveat: the PDF actually downloaded at this URL was a different article (on the Violin Concerto), so this claim rests only on the search snippet.
- **Regions.** In *Structural Functions of Harmony*, Schoenberg defines tonal relationships as "distances" from the tonic, classified and drawn as the "chart of the regions." — [Bernstein, Current Musicology](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/currentmusicology/article/view/4944)
- **Cage's testimony on correction.** In a counterpoint class at UCLA, Schoenberg sent everyone to the blackboard to solve a problem. Cage gave a correct solution; Schoenberg asked for another, then another. When Cage said there were no more, Schoenberg asked him for the principle underlying all the solutions. — [Cage, Indeterminacy story 44](https://www.lcdf.org/indeterminacy/s/44). On Cage's mailing list, members later debated whether the intended answer was voice-leading or consonance/dissonance; Cage never said. — [Silence listserv](https://lists.virginia.edu/sympa/arc/silence/2012-02/msg00010.html)
- **Assistants and editors.**
  - Gerald Strang was Schoenberg's teaching assistant at UCLA (1936–38) and co-edited *Fundamentals* (1967).
  - Newlin, Hoffmann, Stein, Clara Silvers Steuermann and Strang were UCLA pupils who became assistants.
  - Newlin's diaries (*Schoenberg Remembered*, 1980) are described as the first detailed account of his American teaching, including his analysis sessions.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia: Gerald Strang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Strang); [NYRB review](https://www.nybooks.com/?p=25017); [Wikipedia: Dika Newlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dika_Newlin)

### Inferences
- **App drill, "Motive Lab" (constructed example).** Give a basic motive built on an arpeggiated tonic triad, e.g. C5 E5 G5 E5 (quarter, quarter, half, quarter). The student must produce N motive-forms, each tagged with the operator from Schoenberg's list. Examples:
  - rhythm change: C5 E5 G5 (dotted quarter, eighth, half)
  - auxiliary notes: C5 D5 E5 F5 G5
  - order change: G5 E5 C5
  - transposition to II: D5 F5 A5
  - same rhythm with new intervals: C5 F5 A5
  - The app can check which features were preserved (rhythm, contour, intervals) to classify the variation as close or remote.
- **Phrase drill.** Choose motive-forms and assemble a 2-bar phrase, then a sentence: tonic form (I) + dominant form (V), then continuation with liquidation, then cadence. This plugs directly into the existing Caplin sentence/period module.
- **Ch. 9 as auto-feedback rubric.** Checks: highest note placed near the end and not repeated; range limit; no long one-direction runs; bass contour variety; inversion usage.
- **"Give me another solution" mode, after Cage's story.** Once a counterpoint exercise is correct, ask for a second and third distinct correct solution, then ask the student to name the principle they share.

### Gaps
- The often-cited "write 20 (or 50) variations of a motive" quantity was NOT found in the text accessible here. The Fundamentos text says only "many sketches… train each method several times." Treat specific numbers as unverified.
- The exact assignments in *Models for Beginners* Appendix 3 (UCLA Comp. 105) and the "Primer in regions" were not accessible.
- Exercise procedures in *Preliminary Exercises in Counterpoint* and the region exercises in *Structural Functions* were not retrieved.
- Primary testimonies from Newlin, Stein, Strang, Berg and Webern about Schoenberg's correction style were not retrieved. The Stein UCLA reminiscences page returned 503 and the Cambridge chapter is paywalled.
- The exact pitches of the Ex. 17 arpeggio motive were not legible in the text extraction.

## 2. Nadia Boulanger: routine, demands, exercises, sayings

### Takeaway
Boulanger never wrote a method. Her teaching combined:
- keyboard harmony learned by singing and playing: the "Boulanger cadences" memorized in all keys, then Paul Vidal's figured basses and given melodies;
- species and 16th-century counterpoint, leading to canon and fugue;
- solfège in all clefs;
- microscopic analysis of masterworks for their tonal plan;
- choral singing;
- set pieces such as motets and a passacaglia;
- extreme discipline ("loose is loose").

### Cited Findings
- **No textbook.** She tried to publish her method with G. Schirmer in 1925, was refused, and later said: "Writing is not my function. My function is to teach." — [Fick's Music listing, Bonet/Vidal](https://ficksmusic.com/products/a-collection-of-given-basses-and-melodies-dinsic)
- **Harmony at the piano.** She felt harmony had to be taught primarily at the piano. Her favourite tool for serious students was Paul Vidal's figured basses and melodies, later compiled by her pupil Narcís Bonet as a student workbook plus a solutions book. Per Bonet, keyboard progressions and the "Vidal basses" were used "to build reflexes linking hearing to musical thinking." — [Fick's Music](https://ficksmusic.com/products/a-collection-of-given-basses-and-melodies-dinsic); [Bonet, Fundamental Principles of Harmony](https://www.ficksmusic.com/products/the-fundamental-principles-of-harmony-dinsic)
- **The Boulanger Cadences (David Conte, pupil).**
  - At Fontainebleau there was a weekly two-hour class with Annette Dieudonné whose goal was to memorize these cadences in all keys, including modulating to closely related keys and to keys related by simple mixture.
  - Exercises included singing one voice while playing the other three, sometimes swapping hands.
  - Students had to absorb classical voice leading in all keys well enough to realize complex material at sight. "The fingers lead the ear and the mind."
  - Saturday keyboard-harmony class (about six advanced students) included score reading from Stravinsky's *Orpheus* manuscript.
  - Source: [Conte, "Nadia Boulanger, teaching Stravinsky"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/9944395-nadia-boulanger-teaching-stravinsky-to-david-conte?tab=book)
- **Analysis = tonal plan.** For every passage, find the key and how the movement between keys relates to the tonic; "every piece has a tonal plan." Her example: the repeated G's in the first E-minor chord of Stravinsky's *Symphony of Psalms* prepare the G cadence ending the movement, the C-minor double fugue, and the final C-major chord. Her saying: "Music is nothing more than an incalculable number of solutions based on a limited vocabulary." — [Conte](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/9944395-nadia-boulanger-teaching-stravinsky-to-david-conte?tab=book)
- **Present-day reconstruction of her curriculum (EAMA, a Boulanger-lineage school; secondary).**
  - Harmony is "best studied through singing and practice at the piano," starting with basic sequences and cadences before harmonizing figured basses and melodies.
  - Keyboard Harmony I–II: sung/dictated intervals, triads, sevenths; sung cadence and sequence patterns; "the three fundamental root motions" with their voice leading; doubling and soprano-position choice in root position and first inversion.
  - Keyboard Harmony III–V: 6/4, sevenths, ninths, non-chord tones, four-voice melody harmonization, with Vidal and Challan basses and melodies.
  - Musicianship: fixed-do solfège; Bach chorale exercises in multiple clefs (treble, bass, alto, tenor, soprano); dictation including atonal melodies; at advanced level, transposition and score reduction.
  - Counterpoint: species in 2, 3 and 4 voices, then invertible counterpoint, canons (cyclic, by inversion, cancrizans), "flip" chorales, fugue (subject/answer, stretto, augmentation, episodes, middle entries); final project a Prelude and Fugue.
  - Source: [EAMA course descriptions](https://europeanamericanmusicalalliance.org/institute/courses/)
- **Testimonies from pupils.**
  - Ward-Steinman did Palestrina-style exercises "according to Jeppesen." She corrected them anyway ("Then Jeppesen is wrong"), and the library proved her closer to Palestrina.
  - Glass spent five or six hours a day on counterpoint exercises.
  - Copland: "All Mademoiselle's pupils wrote, among other things, motets and a passacaglia."
  - Wednesday-afternoon sessions sang Monteverdi madrigals and Bach motets.
  - She had students sight-read in all clefs, "changing clefs with each measure."
  - Her principle of "la grande ligne" (the long line over harmony).
  - Carter credited her detailed analysis: in simple Bach progressions he hears that "there could have been many other voice-leadings" and the chosen one is meaningful.
  - Sayings: "Loose is not beautiful, loose is loose"; "As long as I am exacting, there is hope. If I am nice, that is a bad sign"; "For now, you must do it my way, later you may do it your way"; "It is not possible to teach composition, only those things which help us appreciate it."
  - She endorsed Stravinsky's "abyss of freedom" maxim. Bar lines had to be drawn with a ruler.
  - Source for all of the above: [Bomberger, JMTP](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/734/1575/1565)
- **Glass.** In his memoir Glass recounts Boulanger finding "hidden fifths" between alto and bass in his work; he studied Bach closely with her. — [classical.net review](https://www.classical.net/music/books/reviews/0871404389a.php); [Berggasse19](https://berggasse19.org/philip-glass-and-la-boulangerie/)
- **Fontainebleau and Piazzolla.** The Conservatoire Américain at Fontainebleau opened in 1921, with Boulanger teaching harmony, counterpoint and composition; Copland was in the first class. She urged Piazzolla to embrace tango rather than abandon it. — [Bach-cantatas bio](https://bach-cantatas.com/Bio/Boulanger-Nadia.htm); [Fondation Etrillard](https://www.fondationetrillard.ch/en/project-new-generation-of-musicians-american-art-schools-of-fontainebleau)

### Inferences
- **App drill, "Cadences in all keys" (constructed example).** Present a 4-voice cadence, e.g. in C: bass C3 F3 G3 C3, soprano E5 F5 D5 C5 (I–IV–V–I). The student must reproduce or spell it in a random key within a time limit. Then add modulation versions (to V, vi, and the mixture key iv/♭VI). Variant: the app plays three voices and the student sings or enters the missing one, matching Conte's "sing one voice, play three."
- **Clef-switching reading drill.** Change clef every bar, as she did in lessons.
- **"Tonal plan" analysis exercise.** Mark the key of each section of a short piece and its relation to the tonic.
- **Vidal-style tasks.** Given figured basses and given melodies (unfigured soprano) to harmonize. These fit the existing outer-voice/figured-bass module.

### Gaps
- The exact chord content of the "Boulanger cadences" set is not in the sources read. Conte mentions that Derek Remes has posted an explanation online; it was not retrieved.
- Primary accounts from Monsaingeon's *Mademoiselle* and Copland's own text were not read directly (cited via Bomberger).
- No reliable source confirms that she required memorization of Bach chorales specifically. The EAMA source mentions Bach chorale exercises in multiple clefs, not memorization.
- Quincy Jones's specific assignments were not found.
- Daily-routine specifics (hours, order of subjects) were not found beyond Glass's 5–6 hours/day of counterpoint.

## 3. Paul Hindemith: Craft of Musical Composition, Book 2 (two-part writing)

### Takeaway
Hindemith's Book 2 is a strictly graded, rule-numbered exercise course. It starts with single unmetred whole-note melodies drawn from the full chromatic scale under tight rules, then adds a second voice, then ornamentation, harmonic "fields" and degree-progression, then free two-part writing with text. Everything must be singable and is verified by singing.

### Cited Findings
- **Chapters (with pages).** I Simplest one-voice melodic patterns (1); II Beginning of two-voice setting (18); III Elaborated melody: auxiliary/returning and passing tones (35); IV suspensions, anticipations, free suspensions (52); V Principles of melodic construction: harmonic cells and fields, step-progression (63); VI neighbour tones approached/left by skip, accented and unaccented free tones (79); VII Tonal higher-units: degree-progression (89); VIII Tonality of the melodies (103); IX Elaboration of the melody model (118); X–XI Free two-voice setting I–II (134, 149). — [Hindemith, Book 2 sample pdf](https://content.e-bookshelf.de/media/reading/L-15194567-cf463a6437.pdf)
- **Preface.** Two-voice work gives "the best opportunity for understanding the hidden principles and activating forces of tone-setting." No prior harmony or counterpoint is needed, but intervals and simple chords are assumed. Every rule/problem is a "challenge [for the teacher] to create new work-material." The method does not force a predetermined style. — [Hindemith Book 2](https://content.e-bookshelf.de/media/reading/L-15194567-cf463a6437.pdf)
- **Chapter I material and conditions.**
  - All 12 chromatic tones from the start; he explicitly rejects limiting beginners to diatonic or church modes.
  - Whole notes only: no metre, no bar-lines; uniform durations of about 2–3 pulse beats.
  - Treble and bass clefs only.
  - Voices are SATB pairs written in the student's own singing range; sung on "la"; text only in the last two chapters.
  - "The final proof of the value of what has been written is always determined by singing each exercise."
  - Source: [Hindemith Book 2](https://content.e-bookshelf.de/media/reading/L-15194567-cf463a6437.pdf)
- **Rules for single-line exercises (verbatim gist).**
  - Rule 1: range about one octave.
  - Rule 2: length roughly 7–14 tones.
  - Rule 3: first note = last note.
  - Rule 4: change direction after at most four tones.
  - Rule 5: only these may precede the final: its 2nd (major or minor) from below or above; its 3rd (major or minor) from above; its 4th from below; its 5th from above.
  - Rule 6: no immediate repetition of a tone; avoid tone–auxiliary–tone figures early on.
  - Rule 7: avoid broken chords (no three successive tones forming a triad), especially tritone-containing groups. Inserting seconds weakens but does not remove the chord effect; judge by ear.
  - Exercise 1: "Construct in whole notes, without bar-lines, several melodies… which you can sing without difficulty. What is difficult to sing, cannot be correct! Keep on improving your work."
  - Exercise 2: check your results against the later rules.
  - Source: [Hindemith Book 2](https://content.e-bookshelf.de/media/reading/L-15194567-cf463a6437.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Constructed Rule-5 example, final C4.** Allowed penultimates are B3/B♭3 (2nd below), D4/D♭4 (2nd above), E4/E♭4 (3rd above), G3 (4th below) and G4 (5th above).
- **Constructed valid melody** (8 tones, chromatic allowed, no triad outline, direction change ≤4, no repeats): C4 D4 F4 E♭4 D♭4 E♭4 D4 C4. Note that two non-adjacent E♭4s do not break Rule 6 as quoted.
- **App value.** This is ideal for an automatic checker: a "Hindemith line" mode with rules 1–7 encoded. It is a chromatic, non-modal counterpart to the cantus-firmus module, and a natural bridge to 2-voice work.

### Gaps
- The concrete rules of Chapter II onward (two-voice intervals, Hindemith's interval-root and "harmonic fluctuation" criteria) were not in the extracted sample.
- *Elementary Training for Musicians* (rhythm/coordination drills) and *Traditional Harmony* (and its exercise format) were not researched within the budget.

## 4. Messiaen, Webern, Ligeti and others (Sessions, Piston, Persichetti, Toch, Kodály)

### Takeaway
The available sources give mostly framing facts, not assignment-level detail:
- Messiaen taught analysis (from 1947) and composition (1966) at the Paris Conservatoire. His pupils included Boulez, Stockhausen and Xenakis. He did not impose his style, and he covered harmony, sound-colour, rhythm, melodic accent and birdsong.
- Webern's 1934–38 private form lectures centred on Beethoven's piano sonatas.
- Ligeti's Hamburg class (1973–89) was a discussion seminar on students' own scores and new music.
- Persichetti's *Twentieth-Century Harmony* ends each chapter with short writing prompts.

### Cited Findings
- **Messiaen's career and pupils.** At the Conservatoire 1941–78: harmony professor in 1941, analysis class in 1947, composition in 1966. Students included Boulez, Xenakis, Pierre Henry, Stockhausen, Méfano, Reverdy and Murail. — [IRCAM biography](https://brahms4.ircam.fr/en/composer/olivier-messiaen/workcourse)
- **Messiaen's class.** He "did not impose his opinions… nor promote imitation of his style." Topics: harmony, sound–colour, rhythm, melodic accentuation, aesthetics; sources include his *Traité de rythme, de couleur, et d'ornithologie*. — [Symposium: "A Creative Legacy: Messiaen as Teacher of Analysis"](https://symposium.music.org/volume-40/articles-1752877058/a-creative-legacy-messiaen-as-teacher-of-analysis) (abstract only; full text returned 403)
- **Webern.** His form courses were given in private Viennese homes 1934–38, and the published notes show his thinking on form "especially as shown in Beethoven's piano sonatas." — [Paul Sacher Stiftung](https://www.paul-sacher-stiftung.ch/en/publications/studies-and-editions/2002-webern-musikalische-formen-en.html)
- **Ligeti in Hamburg.**
  - Professor at the Hochschule für Musik und Theater 1973–89. — [HfMT Hamburg](https://www.hfmt-hamburg.de/en/hochschule/aktuelles/newsroom/newsletter/ligeti-rauf-und-runter-55)
  - Lessons started Wednesdays at 3 pm and often ran into the night at his home. Everyone brought scores; discussion was the format, with Ligeti asking opinions before concluding. Students pursued research (e.g. microtonality, the "microtonal conspiracy") that fed his late works. He delighted in new pieces such as Grisey's *Vortex temporum* and Vivier's *Lonely Child*. — [Stahnke, Circuit 2013](https://www.erudit.org/en/revue/circuit/2013/v23/n2/1018451ar.pdf); [W.-A. Schultz, "Ligeti als Lehrer"](https://wolfgangandreasschultz.de/pdf/Ligeti-Lehrer.pdf)
- **Persichetti.**
  - *Twentieth-Century Harmony* treats chords by thirds, fourths and seconds, added-note chords and polychords. Each chapter ends with a page or two of writing prompts. — [Goodreads / reviews](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/890009)
  - Example prompt (ch. 5, added-note chords): harmonize a given melody for strings with triads in violins/violas and the added notes in cellos/basses in octaves. — [youngcomposers forum](https://www.youngcomposers.com/t48921/persichetti-exercise-5-18-for-chamber-orchestra/)
- **Toch.** *The Shaping Forces in Music* grew from 1944 Harvard lectures (published 1948). It covers harmony, melody, counterpoint and form, with the "wave line" of melody as a central concept and 390 musical examples. — [IMSLP](https://imslp.org/wiki/The_Shaping_Forces_in_Music_(Toch,_Ernst)); [Crescendo listing](https://www.crescendo-music.com/shop/108067-the-shaping-forces-in-music-131254)

### Inferences
- **Persichetti-style "one technique, one short piece" prompts** could add a 20th-century harmony module: quartal voicing, e.g. D4 G4 C5 F5; added-sixth chord C4 E4 G4 A4.
- **A Messiaen-inspired rhythm module** (non-retrogradable rhythms, added values) and modes of limited transposition (mode 2 octatonic: C D♭ E♭ E F♯ G A B♭) are plausible extensions. These are drawn from general knowledge of Messiaen's *Technique de mon langage musical*, NOT from sources read here; verify before use.

### Gaps
- Concrete Messiaen classroom assignments (birdsong dictation, the rhythmic exercises given to students), and Boulez's and Stockhausen's testimonies, were not retrieved.
- Nothing was found on Roger Sessions' teaching methods, Walter Piston's *Harmony*/*Counterpoint* exercise formats, or Kodály's composition-related pedagogy (budget exhausted).
- Toch's specific exercises were not found.

## 5. Schenkerian influence: Salzer & Schachter, Aldwell & Schachter

### Takeaway
*Counterpoint in Composition* (Salzer & Schachter) teaches the species in 2 and 3 parts and then shows them "prolonged" in real compositions. It began as a collection of literature examples, which makes it the clearest model for an app link between species exercises and actual music.

### Cited Findings
- **Structure.**
  - Part 1, elementary counterpoint: cantus firmus and first through fifth species, each in two and three parts.
  - Part 2, "prolonged counterpoint": the direct application of species counterpoint, melodic-contrapuntal prolongations, the chorale, and combined species, ending with voice-leading techniques in historical perspective (c.1450–c.1900).
  - The book began "as a collection of examples from the literature," and its approach derives from Schenker.
  - Source: [Stretta listing](https://www.stretta-music.de/salzer-schachter-counterpoint-in-composition-nr-817022.html); [Crescendo listing](https://www.crescendo-music.com/shop/146674-counterpoint-in-composition-the-study-of-voice-leading-120897)

### Inferences
- **App extension, "species in the wild."** After a 1st–3rd species exercise, show a reduced excerpt where the same species framework underlies real music. Constructed example: a 3rd-species line C5 B4 A4 G4 over a held F3 resembles a passing-tone prolongation within a IV chord. The student identifies the underlying 1st-species skeleton. This links the species module with the existing melody-skeleton/diminution module.

### Gaps
- Specific exercises from *Counterpoint in Composition* and from Aldwell & Schachter's *Harmony and Voice Leading* were not retrieved. No scholarly review of the method's effectiveness was found.

## 6. Adaptable exercise formats for a self-study app (synthesis)

### Takeaway
The most directly adaptable, source-backed formats are:
1. Schoenberg's operator-tagged motive variation, then phrase building, then sentence and period over fixed harmonic frames (I / I–V / I–V–I / I–IV / I–II), with his ch. 9 self-criticism checklist.
2. Hindemith's rule-numbered unmetred chromatic one-voice lines, then two voices, verified by singing.
3. Boulanger's cadences memorized in all keys, with sing-one-play-three, Vidal-style figured basses and given melodies, clef-per-bar reading, and the tonal-plan analysis.
4. Schoenberg's "give another solution… now state the principle" (Cage).
5. Set genre projects: motets, passacaglia (Boulanger); prelude and fugue (EAMA).

### Cited Findings
- Schoenberg's variation operators, phrase-building examples, harmonic frames and self-criticism list — [Fundamentos pdf](https://www.monoskop.org/images/5/50/Schoenberg_Arnold_Fundamentos_da_composicao_musical.pdf)
- Hindemith's Rules 1–7 and Exercises 1–2 — [Hindemith Book 2](https://content.e-bookshelf.de/media/reading/L-15194567-cf463a6437.pdf)
- Boulanger cadences in all keys, with sing/play exchange — [Conte](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/9944395-nadia-boulanger-teaching-stravinsky-to-david-conte?tab=book)
- Copland: everyone wrote motets and a passacaglia; clef change every bar — [Bomberger, JMTP](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/734/1575/1565)
- Cage's multiple-solutions anecdote — [Cage, Indeterminacy](https://www.lcdf.org/indeterminacy/s/44)

### Inferences
Proposed app modules, mapped to their teacher and constructed so that each can be graded automatically:
- **"Motive Lab" (Schoenberg).** Input a motive. The student generates 6–10 forms, each labelled with an operator. The app verifies the preserved and changed features.
- **"Sentence from a motive" (Schoenberg).** Supply a motive and a harmonic frame. Bars 1–2 on I, bars 3–4 on V (dominant form, same rhythm and contour), bars 5–8 with liquidation to a cadence.
- **"Hindemith line" (Hindemith).** Unmetred chromatic cantus-like melody, auto-checked against Rules 1–7. Then a second voice.
- **"Cadence gym" (Boulanger).** One cadence pattern in 12 keys, timed. Variants add modulation to related or mixture keys, and a missing-voice entry.
- **"Clef roulette" (Boulanger).** Read or enter a melody whose clef changes every bar (treble, alto, tenor, soprano, bass).
- **"Second solution" (Schoenberg/Cage).** A correct species exercise unlocks a request for 2 more distinct solutions, then a multiple-choice "what principle do they share?"
- **"Passacaglia project" (Boulanger, Rheinberger).** An 8-bar ground in triple metre; write successive variations over it.

### Gaps
- None of the 20th-century sources consulted gives a ready-made graded exercise sequence beyond what is listed above. Quantities (how many variations, how many keys per session) are design choices, not historical prescriptions.
