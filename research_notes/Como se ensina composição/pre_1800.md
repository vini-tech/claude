## 1. Fux, *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725), and the Viennese Fux line (Haydn → Beethoven, Albrechtsberger, Mozart)

### Takeaway
Fux's method is a graded dialogue. The master, Aloysius, sets a cantus firmus. The pupil, Josephus, writes a counterpoint, and the master corrects it error by error. The order is: species 1→5 in two voices, then three, then four, then imitation, fugue and double counterpoint. In Vienna this remained the backbone of training for 70 years. Mozart used it with Attwood (1785–87), Haydn with Beethoven (1793), and Albrechtsberger with Beethoven (1794–95). Each teacher added something: Mozart tonalized the cantus firmi, and Albrechtsberger annotated each correction with an explanation and ended each unit with a string-quartet piece.

### Cited Findings
- *Gradus ad Parnassum* is written in Latin as a dialogue between a master (Aloysius) and a pupil (Josephus), a teaching format that goes back to the Middle Ages. — [Wikipedia: Johann Joseph Fux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Joseph_Fux); [HistoryofInformation](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=2640)
- Order of content: building a cantus firmus; the five species for two, then three, then four voices; mixed species; fugue in 2, 3 and 4 voices; double counterpoint. — [HistoryofInformation](https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=2640)
- Definition of the species: 1st = one note per CF note; 2nd = two; 3rd = four; 4th = two notes, syncopated (ligatures); 5th = a mixture (florid). — [Project Gutenberg, Treatise on Simple Counterpoint (follows Fux)](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16342/pg16342-images.html), via [search summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterpoint)
- Open Music Theory gives every cantus firmus Fux uses in the *Gradus*, one per mode, in both the upper and lower voice. It recommends practising "any species, on any mode, in both upper and lower parts", with downloadable exercise and solution files. — [Open Music Theory, Intro to Species Counterpoint](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/02%3A_Counterpoint_and_Galant_Schemas/2.01%3A_Introduction_to_Species_Counterpoint); [OMT Nebraska](https://pressbooks.nebraska.edu/openmusictheory/?p=266)
- Mann's English translation (*Steps to Parnassus* / *The Study of Counterpoint*, 1943/1965) covers the preface and pp. 41–139 and 279 of the original, which is the species section. The fugue part came out separately in Mann's *The Study of Fugue* (1958). — [Wikipedia: Alfred Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Mann_(musicologist))
- *The Study of Fugue* (Mann) discusses four 18th-century treatises: Fux (1725), Marpurg (*Abhandlung von der Fuge*, 1753–54), Padre Martini (*Saggio fondamentale pratico di contrappunto fugato*, 1775) and Albrechtsberger (*Gründliche Anweisung zur Composition*, 1790). It has a section on "Albrechtsberger as teacher of Beethoven". — [Mann, The Study of Fugue (description)](https://opencourser.com/book/uqdp0g/the-study-of-fugue)
- **Haydn → Beethoven (1793):** about 245 counterpoint exercises in Beethoven's hand survive, mostly on Fux, some with corrections by Haydn. — [Charles Petzold, Beethoven 250 day 36](https://charlespetzold.com/beethoven250/day036.html)
- The Beethoven-Haus is more sceptical. "Virtually no source material for Haydn's instruction has survived, probably primarily due to the fact that Haydn hardly made any written entries in his pupils' notes." Beethoven seems to have reworked pieces from his Bonn years, and Haydn may have introduced him to "free composition and the art of making compositional sketches". — [Beethoven-Haus Bonn, "In search of the Art of Fugue" guide (PDF)](https://internet.beethoven.de/pdf-sonderausstellung/Auf-der-Suche-nach-der-Kunst-der-Fuge-Brief-Guide.pdf)
- **Albrechtsberger → Beethoven (1794–95):** "Beethoven was obliged to write exercises which were then exhaustively corrected and annotated by Albrechtsberger. [He] not only corrected faults in the musical score but explained his corrections in the margins of the paper together with diagrams and tables to illustrate particular procedures." The three-voice counterpoint sheet carries "a clear list of suitable interval sequences" in tables, and the rules are summarised at the foot of the page. On the double-counterpoint-at-the-tenth exercises the corrections take the form of practical advice: "if you do this, the following happens; you can avoid the pitfall if you change the following". — [Beethoven-Haus guide](https://internet.beethoven.de/pdf-sonderausstellung/Auf-der-Suche-nach-der-Kunst-der-Fuge-Brief-Guide.pdf)
- Structure of the Albrechtsberger course: "First, the pupil was obliged to learn rules and put them into practice in related exercises. As a conclusion to each section of subject matter Beethoven had to work up what he had learnt in a larger piece for string quartet," for example a double fugue for string quartet. In 1794 Beethoven also copied a Haydn quartet from op. 20, apparently as a model for the quartet movements he wrote under Albrechtsberger. — [Beethoven-Haus guide](https://internet.beethoven.de/pdf-sonderausstellung/Auf-der-Suche-nach-der-Kunst-der-Fuge-Brief-Guide.pdf)
- From 1794, and again in 1819, Beethoven copied out Albrechtsberger's passages on treating a fugue subject: augmentation, diminution, inversion and so on. — [Beethoven-Haus guide](https://internet.beethoven.de/pdf-sonderausstellung/Auf-der-Suche-nach-der-Kunst-der-Fuge-Brief-Guide.pdf)
- Lesson frequency with Albrechtsberger: about three times a week for a little over a year (roughly January 1794 to 1795). — [Petzold](https://charlespetzold.com/beethoven250/day036.html) (secondary source)
- Riepel (1752) also took Fux as the reference point, with more than 70 mentions in the *Anfangsgründe*. But he "modernised" Fux's modal examples by rereading them in major and minor (Dorian read as D minor) and recasting them as thoroughbass sequence models. His student-character says: "You advised me to imitate all of Fux's examples…" — [Oliver Wiener, GMTH Proceedings 2002 (PDF)](https://storage.gmth.de/proceedings/articles/219/pdf/gmth-proceedings-artikel-219.pdf)

### Fux's cantus firmi (for the app) **[unverified in this session; check against Mann 1965 / OMT]**
These are the six CFs as usually transcribed from Mann's translation. The first, the Dorian, is the main example of the treatise:
- Dorian: D4 F4 E4 D4 G4 F4 A4 G4 F4 E4 D4
- Phrygian: E4 C4 D4 C4 A3 A4 G4 E4 F4 E4
- Lydian: F4 G4 A4 F4 D4 E4 F4 C5 A4 F4 G4 F4
- Mixolydian: G3 C4 B3 G3 C4 E4 D4 G4 E4 C4 D4 B3 A3 G3
- Aeolian: A3 C4 B3 D4 C4 E4 F4 E4 D4 C4 B3 A3
- Ionian: C4 E4 F4 G4 E4 A4 G4 E4 F4 E4 D4 C4

### Inferences
- Fux's correction loop maps directly onto an app. The pupil submits, the master points out each error with its rule ("here you have two fifths", "this leap is not singable"), and the pupil rewrites. Albrechtsberger adds the useful element of the explained correction ("if you do X, Y happens; change Z"), plus a table of good interval sequences shown before the exercise.
- Albrechtsberger's "unit → final piece" design suggests a capstone after each block. For example, after 1st–3rd species, write a short two-voice piece (an invention or minuet) that uses those techniques.
- Every Fux CF works in both upper and lower voice, across 5 species and 6 modes: 6 × 2 × 5 = 60 exercise slots for two voices, and more for 3 and 4 voices.

### Gaps
- I could not see Fux's dialogue text directly (the specific error-by-error corrections, such as the famous "Why did you write a fifth here?"). The Mann translation is not openly available.
- I found no precise list of which exercises Haydn corrected in Beethoven's work, or what he wrote. The Beethoven-Haus account and the "245 exercises" figure partly conflict.

## 2. Neapolitan conservatories: partimento, regola dell'ottava, solfeggi

### Takeaway
The Neapolitan curriculum ran from bass to music. Students learned **cadences** (simple → compound → double), then the **scale/rule of the octave** in all keys and in the three positions, then **dissonances/suspensions**, then **bass motions** (sequential *moti del basso*). After that came figured partimenti, then unfigured ones, then diminished, imitative and fugal partimenti, the summit of the course. Fenaroli spread the material over graded books, with book 4 the "advanced partimento course". Students started as children, around 10–11, and finished writing four-voice counterpoint.

### Cited Findings
- Order: "students at the Neapolitan conservatories were immediately taught the rule of the octave [after cadences] … The term 'rule of the octave' was not used in 18th-century Italy — this rule was referred to as *scale*." — [Sanguinetti review / summary (Therese de Goede, PDF)](https://www.theresedegoede.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Review_Giorgio_Sanguinetti_The_Art_of_Partiment.pdf); see also [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Sanguinetti, *The Art of Partimento* (2012), Part Two, groups the theory into classes: (I) basic axioms, (II) rule of the octave, (III) suspensions, (IV) bass motions, (V) [cut off in the source]. Partimento theory treats only suspensions as dissonances. — [de Goede review](https://www.theresedegoede.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Review_Giorgio_Sanguinetti_The_Art_of_Partiment.pdf)
- Manuscripts vary in order. A Naples MS starts with cadences and then the rule of the octave in remote keys. A Palermo MS opens with the rule of the octave in major and minor, in common and remote keys. — [de Goede review](https://www.theresedegoede.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Review_Giorgio_Sanguinetti_The_Art_of_Partiment.pdf)
- Fenaroli's *Regole musicali per i principianti di cembalo* (1775; editions until 1930) is organised around four themes: rule of the octave, cadences, suspensions and bass motions. — [Orfeu/UDESC (Sépe/Fenaroli)](https://www.periodicos.udesc.br/index.php/orfeu/article/view/25505); [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Fenaroli's graded books: once books 1–3 had been absorbed, "the student could proceed with book 4, which was clearly intended by Fenaroli as his advanced partimento course". Fenaroli advises doing books 1 and 2 before the *moti del basso* of book 3, for anyone studying counterpoint. — [Cambridge, Eighteenth-Century Music, "On Fedele Fenaroli's pedagogy: an update"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/eighteenth-century-music/article/on-fedele-fenarolis-pedagogy-an-update/9624A245D7BA5FBC4BD85CE980A6B8B1); [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Typical treatise sequence: rules, then exercises of rising difficulty (figured bass → unfigured bass), then imitative partimenti, then partimento fugues, "the culmination of training" (Gjerdingen 2020, p. 122). — [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- An 1878 statement from an Italian academy describes the old practice: students studied the rule of the octave and "practiced it in the three different positions until having attained proficiency" before moving on to partimenti. — [Gettysburg, The Rule of the Octave](https://musictheory.sites.gettysburg.edu/?p=1533)
- Teachers and collections: A. Scarlatti; his pupils Durante and Leo; Fenaroli, Furno, Insanguine (*regole*); Sala, Tritto, Mattei, Paisiello, Cotumacci, Cafaro, Zingarelli. The earliest dated collection is attributed to Pasquini (c. 1700–10). Handel used partimenti with Princess Anne. The "Langloz manuscript" may reflect Bach's method. — [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Furno's rule by scale degree: ① 3 & 5; ② minor 3, 4, major 6 (6/4/3); ③ 3 & 6; ④ 3 & 5, adding a 6 when rising to ⑤ (6/5/3); ⑤ major 3 & 5, with a minor 7 resolving to the 3rd of ①; ⑥ 3 & 6 (or 6/4/3 when treated like ②); ⑦ 3 & 6, with a diminished 5 when rising to ① (6/5/3). — [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Fenaroli's cadences: **simple** (⑤ 5/3 → ① 5/3); **compound** (a 4 over ⑤, prepared by the octave of ①, resolving to the major 3: 4–3); **double** (over ⑤: 5/3 – 6/4 – 5/4 – 5/3). Consonances are 3, 5, 6 and 8. Dissonances are 2, 4, 7 and 9, which must be prepared and resolved. Parallel fifths and octaves are forbidden. — [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Bass motions catalogued: up a 3rd / down a step; down a 3rd / up a step; up or down a 4th/5th; up a 6th / down a 5th; by semitone; descending with ties. — [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Diminution turns a slow melody into a fast, florid one. Imitation, "the right hand answering or anticipating the bass motive" (Tomeoni, 1795), prepares the student for fugue. — [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Age and duration: students started around 10–11 and ended writing "complicated four-voice counterpoint full of distant modulations and chromatic embellishments". Gjerdingen (*Child Composers in the Old Conservatories*, 2020) reconstructed the lost daily lessons from conservatory archives. — [Goodreads description](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/49046502); [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)
- Attwood spent two years in Naples before going to Mozart in Vienna (1785). — [Interlude.hk](https://interlude.hk/sorcerers-apprentice-mozarts-english-gentleman-student/)

### Rule of the octave in C major (Fenaroli-style form, for the app) **[standard textbook form; matches the Furno degrees above]**
- Ascending bass: C3 D3 E3 F3 G3 A3 B3 C4. Figures: 5/3 – 6/4/3 – 6/3 – 6/5/3 – 5/3 – 6/3 – 6/5/3 – 5/3
- Descending bass: C4 B3 A3 G3 F3 E3 D3 C3. Figures: 5/3 – 6/3 – #6/4/3 – 5/3 – 6/4/2 – 6/3 – 6/4/3 – 5/3
- One possible upper voice for the ascending form (soprano starting on the 3rd): E4 F4 G4 A4 B4 C5 D5 E5. Practise it in all three positions (top voice on the 3rd, the 5th or the octave), as in the 1878 Gettysburg account.

### Inferences
- An app track could follow: (1) cadences in 3 forms × 3 positions × keys; (2) rule of the octave ascending and descending, in 3 positions, in all keys; (3) 7–6, 4–3 and 9–8 suspensions over given basses; (4) *moti del basso* as sequences (up 4th/down 3rd, descending 5ths, etc.); (5) short figured partimenti; (6) unfigured ones, where the student infers the figures from the rule of the octave; (7) diminution of the realisation; (8) imitative partimenti and partimento fugue.
- This fits naturally alongside the app's existing outer-voice harmony and galant schemata: the schemata are the "melodic parts" of the same basses.

### Gaps
- Partimenti.org (Monuments of Partimenti) blocked access, so I have no transcribed partimenti (e.g. Fenaroli Book 1 no. 1) and no description of the solfeggi.
- Daily routine, the role of the *maestrino* (older student teaching younger ones), and the total years of study were not confirmed in any accessible source.

## 3. J.S. Bach as a teacher

### Takeaway
Bach skipped the Fux species altogether. His order was pure four-part thoroughbass, then chorales: first he gave the bass and the pupil added alto and tenor, then the pupil invented the bass for a given melody. Fugue came later. The bass comes before the inner voices, and the realisation is written out in full parts.

### Cited Findings
- C.P.E. Bach to Forkel (1775): "In composition [J. S. Bach] started his pupils right in with what was practical, and omitted all the dry species of counterpoint that were given by Fux and others. His pupils had to begin their studies by learning pure four-part thoroughbass. From this he went to chorales; first he added the basses to them himself, and they had to invent the alto and tenor. Then he taught them to devise the basses themselves. He particularly insisted on the writing out of the thoroughbass in parts." (*New Bach Reader*, p. 399) — [Remeš, MTO 26.3 (2020), note 8](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.remes.pdf)
- Remeš's dissertation summary describes three areas in Bach's teaching: thoroughbass, chorale and fugue. The "fundamental rules" of composition derive from thoroughbass at the keyboard. Bach left only two brief sets of thoroughbass rules. The *Vorschriften und Grundsätze* (1738, "Precepts and Principles") cannot be securely attributed to him. — [Remeš, ZGMTH 2019 / dissertation](https://storage.gmth.de/zgmth/pdf/997); [MTO dissertation index](https://mtosmt.org/docs/diss-index.php?id=579)
- A close parallel is G.H. Stölzel's method (*Kurzer und gründlicher Unterricht*, c. 1719–49; Stölzel was Bach's contemporary). (1) The pupil composes **his own** chorale melody, following three rules: begin on scale degree 1, 3 or 5; end on degree 1; move mostly by step, sometimes by a 3rd or 4th, very rarely more than a 4th. (2) Phrases modulate to the normal keys: iii, V and vi in major; III and v in minor (taken from Heinichen 1711). (3) Invent the bass below each melody degree using default intervals of 3, 5 or 8, where degrees with a default 5 take an alternative 8 and vice versa. (4) Write the whole bass before adding alto and tenor. "Like J. S. Bach's method … one first composes the bass line in its entirety before adding middle voices." — [Remeš, MTO 26.3](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.remes.pdf)
- The "Choralbuch" tradition (melody + figured bass) around Bach's pupils is presented as Bach's pedagogical paradigm. Remeš edited *Realizing Thoroughbass Chorales in the Circle of J. S. Bach* (2019). — [Remeš, MTO 26.3, bibliography](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.remes.pdf)

### Inferences
- An app sequence along Bach's lines: (a) realise a four-voice figured bass and write it out in full; (b) chorale with soprano and bass given, filling in alto and tenor; (c) chorale with only the soprano given, inventing the bass first (outer voices) and then filling in; (d) optionally, Stölzel-style, compose the melody too.
- This fits the app's "outer-voice harmony" module well, and adds the next step of filling the inner voices.

### Gaps
- I did not access the texts of Kellner or the *Vorschriften* directly, or Kirnberger's/Gerber's testimony about Bach. Nor did I find sources on the Inventions and the WTC as teaching works (beyond the well-known title page of the Inventions, which I did not verify in this session).

## 4. Mozart as teacher (Attwood, 1785–87) and as pupil

### Takeaway
Mozart's course for Attwood (Attwood MS, K. 506a; NMA X/30/1) ran August 1785 to February 1787 and covered figured bass, Fux counterpoint (more than 100 pages), canon and fugue, and free composition. The figured-bass lessons were probably the first step. They are graded by chord type, have no rhythm, and Mozart corrects them **melodically**: he reshapes the top voice into a singing arch.

### Cited Findings
- The manuscript runs August 1785 – February 1787, with 141 leaves and 267 written sides. It contains rudiments, figured bass, counterpoint "in the manner of Fux", free composition, and some orchestration and text underlay. — [Schellhous, JMTP (PDF)](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- The figured-bass lessons "were probably undertaken first" and contain more of Mozart's handwriting than any other part. Only **two** chord types are taught, the common chord and the seventh chord; all others are derived from them (via Kirnberger/Rameau). — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- **Exact sequence of the figured basses:** 5 exercises in root-position triads only → 5 adding the 6 chord → 6 adding 6/4 → 4 with seventh chords (all inversions at once) → 3 with ninth chords. Each set opens with instructions in Mozart's hand on the new chord. — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- Design: no rhythm, no regular phrasing, no minor-key exercises (though minor keys appear inside them). All start on a C major triad and end, sometimes abruptly, on almost any degree, as well as on E♭, C♯ and G♯. The first exercises (AS 8–9) systematically drill every root progression; AS 9 uses all 12 notes and a chain of fifths with a figure changing from minor 3rd to major 3rd. — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- Attwood's difficulty and Mozart's correction: Attwood wrote, "I don't know how it is possible to put the 5th here." His revised version was correct "but at the cost of a directionless melodic line". Mozart's correction gives the top voice a climax (an F♯ approached chromatically) and a half-step descent to E and then to the leading tone. Mozart asks for a "singing" line (cantilena), requires dissonances to resolve downward, and ties notes in the soprano for continuity. Melody comes first, while keeping four voices where possible. — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- Mozart's demonstration (AS 5) orders the harmonies related to C from nearest to most remote: G – A – F – E – D. Attwood's note reads: "By the above rule one easily sees what modulations are best and most natural." — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- Counterpoint: more than half the manuscript is counterpoint. Mozart altered Fux's cantus firmi and composed new ones, "more conjunct and symmetrical … more clearly directed toward the fifth of the mode" (Mann, KB 27), which brings them closer to tonal style. — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- The move to florid writing goes through **embellishment patterns for suspensions** (AS 68–69): "Free writing is to emerge only gradually from embellishment techniques." In free composition, Attwood harmonises a **little march tune** several times (AS 175–180), keeping continuous eighths or sixteenths in one voice against slower voices. Mozart's corrections add suspensions, inversions and chromaticism. — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- The segments (figured bass / counterpoint / free composition) may have been worked in alternation rather than one after another, judging by the ink and handwriting. — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- Mozart started Attwood on two-voice species exercises after Fux, spanning more than 100 pages. Wesley's anecdote has Mozart telling Attwood: "Do not be too much in a hurry — study plain counterpoint for about twelve months" (before fugue). — [Interlude.hk](https://interlude.hk/sorcerers-apprentice-mozarts-english-gentleman-student/)
- Conflict: Interlude says the course began with species counterpoint, while Schellhous thinks the figured basses came first. The alternating-segments hypothesis would reconcile the two. — [Interlude.hk](https://interlude.hk/sorcerers-apprentice-mozarts-english-gentleman-student/) vs. [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- Mozart as a pupil: on 9 October 1770 in Bologna he sat the Accademia Filarmonica exam, setting the antiphon *Quaerite primum* (K. 86) in strict stile antico. Padre Martini corrected the manuscript, and his annotations survive. Martini's certificate is dated 12 October. Fringe claims that Martini wrote the piece are not confirmed. — [King's College, Mozart & Material Culture](https://mmc.kdl.kcl.ac.uk:443/entities/person/martini-giovanni-battista-1706-1784); [Wikipedia: Grabmusik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grabmusik)

### Inferences
- "Mozart–Attwood" exercises for the app: (1) figured basses **without rhythm**, graded by chord type (5/3 → 6 → 6/4 → 7 and inversions → 9), always starting in C and ending on new degrees, which trains modulation from the start; (2) correction focused on the **top voice's shape** (a single climax, an arch, a chromatic approach to the high point), not just on rule errors; (3) after strict counterpoint, a catalogue of embellishment patterns for suspensions as the bridge to florid writing; (4) rewrite the same short tune (the "march tune") several times with different accompaniment textures.
- Mozart's tonalised CFs (stepwise, symmetrical, aimed at the 5th) suggest the app should offer "tonal CFs" alongside Fux's modal ones.

### Gaps
- I did not have the NMA X/30/1 musical examples, so no note-by-note transcription of the AS exercises (e.g. AS 9's bass) is given here.
- No accessible source covered Leopold Mozart's method (the Nannerl notebook) or Wolfgang copying models (e.g. the "London Notebook").

## 5. Phrase-construction treatises: Riepel, Koch, Kirnberger

### Takeaway
Riepel (1752–) and Koch (1782–93) teach melody as a syntax. The basic exercise is the **minuet**, first written in a "model form" (regular 4+4 phrases with standard cadences), then expanded with repetition, appendices/suffixes, insertions and cadence extensions. Riepel names the second-half continuation patterns **Monte, Fonte and Ponte**. Kirnberger's *Der allezeit fertige Polonoisen- und Menuettencomponist* (1757) makes minuets combinatorially, one bar per throw of the dice.

### Cited Findings
- Riepel, *Anfangsgründe zur musicalischen Setzkunst*, chapter I "De rhythmopoeia, oder von der Tactordnung" (Regensburg/Vienna 1752; 2nd ed. 1754). It works with "visible examples" rather than in the mathematical manner. English translation of chapters 1–2 by John Walter Hill (Pendragon, 2014). — [Encyclopedia.com: Riepel](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/dictionaries-thesauruses-pictures-and-press-releases/riepel-joseph); [Hill translation (UCI PDF)](https://music.arts.uci.edu/abauer/5.2/readings/Joseph_Riepel_John_Walter_Hill_trans.Tonordnung_2015.pdf)
- Riepel names three continuation patterns for the second half of a minuet after their contours. **Fonte** (fountain) is a descending sequence: a two-bar unit repeated a step lower (D7/ii → ii, then D7 → I). **Monte** (mountain) is ascending (D7/IV → IV, D7/V → V). **Ponte** (bridge) need not be a sequence; it creates delay, prolonging the dominant, rather than motion. These are discussed in chapters 2–4. There is no consensus on how to interpret the terms (Eckert 2004). — [GMTH Proceedings 301 (Eckert)](https://www.gmth.de/proceedings/artikel/301.aspx); [Open Music Theory 5.14: Galant Schemata – Continuation Patterns](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_1e_(Wharton_and_Shaffer_Eds)/05%3A_Form_(II)_-_Other_Forms_and_Thematic_Structures/5.14%3A_Galant_Schemata_-_Continuation_Patterns)
- Riepel's chapter 3 (*Gründliche Erklärung der Tonordnung insbesondere*, 1757) builds every 3- and 4-voice chord on the diatonic scale combinatorially, using the figured bass as a "combinatorial programme". He works "with small, versatile theory segments, starting from examples". — [Wiener, GMTH 2002](https://storage.gmth.de/proceedings/articles/219/pdf/gmth-proceedings-artikel-219.pdf)
- Koch, *Versuch einer Anleitung zur Composition* (Leipzig 1782, 1787, 1793). Vol. 2 covers melody and its "mechanical rules"; vol. 3 covers "the combination of the melodic parts, or the structure of periods". It is the "first book to deal with the systematic and detailed structure of harmony, melody and musical syntax." — [Wikipedia: Heinrich Christoph Koch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Christoph_Koch)
- Baker's English translation (Yale, 1983) covers Part 2, section 3 ("the nature of the melodic sections") and section 4 ("the connection of melodic sections"). — [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1130000794065252992)
- Koch's minuet pedagogy (Belcher & Thurmaier): the student first writes a basic, unembellished minuet ("model form") and then expands it into an "elaborated form" through phrase-expansion techniques. Many examples come from Haydn; others are Koch's own. — [Haydn journal (Berklee) abstract](https://remix.berklee.edu/haydn-journal/vol10/iss2/2)
- Extension methods illustrated in Koch (per an index description): (1) cadence extension; (2) repetition, exact or sequential, within the phrase; (3) augmentation (lengthening note values). — [search result, ideals.illinois.edu](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/72608) (summary only)
- Kirnberger, *Der allezeit fertige Polonoisen- und Menuettencomponist* (Berlin, Winter, 1757). Each throw of the die picks the bar for that position from a table. One die is used: 6 options per bar position, possibly 12 with two dice. There are separate tables for "Erster Theil" and "Zweyter Theil". The material is 154 bars for the polonaise (D major), 96 for the minuet and 96 for the trio. Similar later works: C.P.E. Bach (1758, six bars of double counterpoint) and Stadler (1780, minuets and trios). — [Graz scan (PDF)](https://imsc.uni-graz.at/fripertinger/DiskrM1314/menuet.pdf); [Interlude: Musical Dice Games](https://interlude.hk/musical-dice-games/)

### Inferences
- Koch drill for the app: (1) give a model 8-bar minuet (4+4: half cadence, then authentic cadence); (2) ask for a single expansion technique (repeat bar 2, add a cadential suffix, extend the cadence, augment) and check that the result keeps the same skeleton; (3) combine techniques up to 10–12 bars. This connects directly to the existing Caplin sentence/period module.
- Riepel drill: after the double bar of the minuet, choose Monte, Fonte or Ponte to return to the tonic. The app already has schemata, so this would be a "schema in formal position" exercise.
- Dice-game drill: show 6 interchangeable bars per position (all over the same harmony) to teach that a bar's harmonic function matters more than its surface. A useful variation: the student writes a 7th option for one bar position.

### Gaps

So there are no exact definitions of Koch's terms (*Einschnitt*, *Absatz*, *Grundabsatz*, *Quintabsatz*, *Schlusssatz*, *Anhang*, *Parenthese*) and no note-by-note examples from Riepel's minuets. These terms are widely discussed in Baker, Sisman ("Small and Expanded Forms: Koch's Model and Haydn's Music", 1982) and Lester (1992).

## 6. What this suggests for designing progressive exercises (synthesis)

### Takeaway
The four traditions agree on three principles. (1) Start from **reduced** material: no rhythm (Mozart's figured basses), whole notes (Fux), or an unadorned model (Koch). (2) Advance by **adding one variable at a time**: one species, one chord type, one cadence type, one expansion technique. (3) End each block with a **complete piece** that applies it (Albrechtsberger's string quartet, Fenaroli's partimento fugue, Koch's elaborated minuet). Corrections were individual and explained, and they covered melodic quality as well as rule-breaking.

### Cited Findings
- Graded sequence by chord type in Mozart's lessons (5/3 → 6 → 6/4 → 7 → 9), with no rhythm. — [Schellhous](https://journals.ou.edu/jmtp/article/download/835/1676/1666)
- Cadences → scale → dissonances → bass motions → figured partimenti → unfigured → imitative/fugal. — [Wikipedia: Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento); [de Goede](https://www.theresedegoede.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Review_Giorgio_Sanguinetti_The_Art_of_Partiment.pdf)
- Rules → exercises → larger piece at the end of each section (Albrechtsberger), with written explanations of the corrections. — [Beethoven-Haus](https://internet.beethoven.de/pdf-sonderausstellung/Auf-der-Suche-nach-der-Kunst-der-Fuge-Brief-Guide.pdf)
- Four-part thoroughbass → chorale with bass given → chorale inventing the bass (Bach). — [Remeš, MTO](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.20.26.3/mto.20.26.3.remes.pdf)
- Model form → elaborated form (Koch). — [Haydn journal abstract](https://remix.berklee.edu/haydn-journal/vol10/iss2/2)

### Inferences (concrete proposals)
1. **"Mozart-style" correction**: besides flagging errors, the app proposes an improved version of the top voice (single climax, an arch, resolutions downward) and explains why, as Albrechtsberger did ("if you do X, Y happens").
2. **Rhythmless figured bass with modulating endings**: start in C and end on V, vi, IV or iii, so that key relationships are taught early.
3. **Rule-of-the-octave track** (3 positions × 12 keys) as a prerequisite for unfigured partimenti.
4. **Bach chorale track**: soprano + bass → fill in alto and tenor; then soprano only → bass first.
5. **Koch/Riepel track**: 8-bar model minuet → expansions → second half with Monte, Fonte or Ponte → complete minuet.
6. **Dice game** to teach harmonic equivalence between bars.
7. **Tonal CFs** (Mozart) alongside modal ones (Fux). Suspension-embellishment patterns serve as the bridge to 4th and 5th species and to free writing.
