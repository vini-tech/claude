## 1. How much space does each topic get? (curriculum weight map)

### Takeaway
Modern curricula follow the same skeleton:
1. fundamentals
2. diatonic triads and voice leading, about 30% of chapters and the largest single block
3. diatonic sevenths
4. Chromaticism I: secondary functions and modulation
5. Chromaticism II: mixture, Neapolitan, augmented 6ths, enharmonic, late-19th-century

Topics like the dominant ninth, the augmented triad and 11th/13th chords get a section, never a chapter. Diatonic voice leading, function, cadences and non-chord tones dominate. Secondary dominants are the single most important chromatic topic.

## 2. Diatonic foundations: triads, inversions, doubling, four-part voice leading, functions, root motion, cadences

### Takeaway
The strict core is the same everywhere:
- avoid parallel and direct (hidden) perfect 5ths and 8ves
- keep adjacent upper voices within an octave
- resolve the leading tone up and chordal sevenths down
- double the root of root-position triads; never double the leading tone

Progressions run T → PD → D → T, with root motion by descending 5th as the prototype. Rameau and Sechter derived everything from a fundamental bass. Schoenberg ranked root motions as:
- **strong (ascending):** a 4th up or a 3rd down
- **descending:** a 4th down or a 3rd up
- **superstrong:** by step

### Cited Findings
- **Schoenberg's three classes of root progression:**
  - "strong or ascending" (a 4th up or a 3rd down) "can be used without restriction"
  - "descending" (a 4th down or a 3rd up) is "better used in combinations of three chords" that together make a strong progression
  - "superstrong" (by 2nd) "often appear as deceptive" and "may be considered too strong for continuous use"
  - Source: [Meeus, MTO 6.1 (2000)](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.00.6.1/mto.00.6.1.meeus.html)
- **Rameau's view, per Meeus:** Rameau's theory "is not about chord inversion, but about chord progressions". Tonality is governed by the fundamental bass. Progressions down a 5th or 3rd are preferable to those up. The *double emploi* explains some step progressions as substitutes for 4th progressions. — [Meeus, MTO](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.00.6.1/mto.00.6.1.meeus.html)
- **Sechter's and Bruckner's view:** Bruckner, following Sechter, taught that progressions by 2nd are often "scheinbare Schritte" (apparent progressions) with an elided intermediate root. For example, IV–V is heard as IV–(ii)–V. — [Meeus, MTO](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.00.6.1/mto.00.6.1.meeus.html)
- **Meeus merges the categories into two vectors:**
  - Dominant: a 5th down, or substitutes a 3rd down / 2nd up.
  - Subdominant: a 5th up, or a 3rd up / 2nd down.
  - Source: [Meeus, MTO](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.00.6.1/mto.00.6.1.meeus.html)
- **Meeus' frequency data.** In Bach's chorale BWV 321, 23 of 25 progressions (92%) are dominant-type, about 30% of them substitutions. In Lassus' chanson *Bon jour mon coeur*, 64% are dominant. Meeus calls the Bach figures typical of common practice but warns that two or three examples are not conclusive. — [Meeus, MTO](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.00.6.1/mto.00.6.1.meeus.html)
- **Fenaroli's rule of the octave (four voices, major).**
  - Ascending: 1 = 5/3, 2 = 6/4/3, 3 = 6/3, 4 = 6/5/3, 5 = 5/3, 6 = 6/3, 7 = 6/5/3, 8 = 5/3.
  - Descending: 8 = 5/3, 7 = 6/3, 6 = 6/4/3, 5 = 5/3, 4 = 4/2, 3 = 6/3, 2 = 6/4/3, 1 = 5/3.
  - Source: [Gettysburg Counterpoint Resources, "The Rule of the Octave" (after Fenaroli 1775, voice leading after Remeš)](https://musictheory.sites.gettysburg.edu/?p=1533)
- **Rule of the octave, other points:**
  - Only degrees 1 and 5 carry root-position triads.
  - In minor, degrees 6 and 7 are raised when the bass ascends and lowered when it descends.
  - The rule is a default, not universal; degrees 4 and 6 are the main exceptions.
  - The term "rule of the octave" was not used in 18th-century Italy (they said "scales").
  - Source: [Wikipedia, Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento); [essaysonmusic, rule of the octave in minor per Fenaroli](https://essaysonmusic.com/?p=27023)
- **Kostka-Payne chapter coverage:** voice leading and root-position part writing get two chapters. First- and second-inversion triads get one chapter each. Cadences and phrase get one chapter. — [McGraw-Hill TOC](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0078025141/information_center_view0/table_of_contents.html)

### Inferences

**Triads and inversions [std]**
- **(a) What it is.** Triads in major and minor; 5/3, 6/3 and 6/4 positions.
- **(b) Rules.**
  - Root position: double the root.
  - First inversion: doubling is freer. Double soprano or bass, or the most stable tone; never the leading tone.
  - Diminished triads (vii°6): usually first inversion, with the 3rd (the bass) doubled.
  - The 6/4 is dissonant and appears in four types only:
    - cadential: I6/4 standing for V's 6/4 suspension
    - passing: e.g. I – V6/4 – I6 over bass C3 D3 E3
    - neighbor/pedal: I – IV6/4 – I over a C3 pedal
    - arpeggiating
  - In the 6/4, double the bass (the 5th of the chord).
- **(c) Weight.** Very high. It is foundational; Aldwell-Schachter gives the 6/4 techniques a whole part.
- **(d) Breaks.**
  - Free 6/4 chords appear in Romantic music, e.g. Brahms; root-position-free 6/4 sonorities appear in Debussy.
  - In late-Romantic and Impressionist music, chords are treated as colors rather than inversions of functional chords.

**Four-part voice leading [std]**
- **(a) What it is.** SATB writing with these ranges:
  - S C4–G5
  - A G3–D5
  - T C3–G4
  - B E2–C4
- **(b) Rules.**
  - S–A and A–T no more than an octave apart; T–B may be wider.
  - No voice crossing or overlap.
  - No parallel P5/P8/unisons, including by contrary motion ("consecutive" 5ths).
  - No direct/hidden 5ths or 8ves into the outer voices unless the soprano moves by step.
  - The leading tone in an outer voice must rise to the tonic.
  - The chordal 7th must resolve down by step.
  - Avoid augmented 2nds (e.g. F4–G#4 in A minor) and augmented 4ths melodically.
  - Prefer common tones and stepwise motion; contrary motion against the bass.
- **(b) Example [recon], C major, I–IV–V–I.**

  | Voice | I | IV | V | I |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | S | E5 | F5 | D5 | C5 |
  | A | C5 | C5 | B4 | C5 |
  | T | G4 | A4 | G4 | E4 |
  | B | C3 | F3 | G3 | C3 |

  - In the V–I, the leading tone B4 rises to C5 in the alto, landing in unison with the soprano. The alternative is B4 → G4, a "frustrated leading tone" in an inner voice, which is common in Bach and gives a complete final triad.
  - **Flag:** reconstructed example; check it before app use.
- **(c) Weight.** Highest of all. Every curriculum front-loads it, and Richter and Dubois are essentially rule books for it.
- **(d) Breaks.** Each break is listed with what it accomplishes.
  - **"Mozart fifths."** The Ger+6 resolving straight to V produces parallel P5s, accepted in Classical practice.
  - **Frustrated leading tone.** In inner voices in Bach chorales, the leading tone leaps down to 5 to complete the final tonic triad.
  - **Planing.** Debussy's parallel chords, e.g. "La cathédrale engloutie" and the Sarabande from *Pour le piano*. The chord becomes timbre, doubling the melody; it rejects voice-leading independence and functional pull.
  - **Unresolved sevenths.** Satie's *Gymnopédie* no. 1 (Gmaj7–Dmaj7 alternation) leaves the dissonance as static color.
  - **Rock/pop power-chord parallels.** These are outside this scope.

**Harmonic function and progression [std + cited]**
- **(a) What it is.** Tonic (I, vi, sometimes iii), predominant (IV, ii, ii6, ii7; later N6 and the augmented 6ths), dominant (V, V7, vii°, vii°7). The phrase model is T–PD–D–T.
- **(b) Rules.**
  - "Progression" follows T → PD → D → T.
  - "Retrogression" (D → PD, e.g. V–IV) is avoided in strict style. Exception: a passing IV6 between V and V6 (bass G2 – A2 – B2).
  - Root motion by descending 5th is the norm (cited Meeus/Rameau). Descending 3rds subdivide it, e.g. I–vi–IV–ii.
  - In strict style, iii is weak and usually goes to vi or IV.
- **(b) Example [recon], C major, I–vi–IV–ii–V–I.**
  - Bass C3 A2 F2 D3 G2 C3 (descending-3rds then descending-5ths root motion).
  - Soprano E5 E5 F5 F5 D5 C5 is a reconstruction; check it for parallels before use.
- **(c) Weight.** Very high. A dedicated chapter in Kostka (ch. 7) and Puget Sound (ch. 9).
- **(d) Breaks.**
  - Schoenberg's superstrong progressions, e.g. V–vi as a deceptive progression, are used "sparingly" for emphasis.
  - V–IV retrogression is common in blues and rock.
  - In late-Romantic music, function is carried by voice leading rather than root motion, e.g. Wagner's *Tristan* chord (F3–B3–D#4–G#4) "resolving" to E7 (V7 of A minor). The tonic is never sounded in the opening, so function is prolonged without arrival.
  - Brahms often uses plagal (IV–I) cadences. ← unverified generalization.

**Cadences [std]**
- **(a) Types.**
  - PAC: V(7)–I in root position, soprano on 1.
  - IAC: inverted chord, or soprano on 3/5.
  - HC: ends on V.
  - Phrygian HC: iv6–V in minor, bass a half step down 6→5.
  - Plagal: IV–I.
  - Deceptive: V–vi.
  - The cadential 6/4 is I6/4–V with 6–5 and 4–3 over a sustained bass 5.
- **(b) Example, PAC with cadential 6/4 [recon], C major (I6/4 – V(7) – I):**

  | Voice | I6/4 | V(7) | I |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | S | C5 | B4 | C5 |
  | A | G4 | G4 (F4 in V7) | E4 |
  | T | E4 | D4 | C4 |
  | B | G2 | G2 | C3 |

  - Rules: the 6/4 falls on the stronger beat than the V; double the bass; the 6 and 4 resolve down.
  - **Deceptive:** in the V–vi, double the 3rd of vi so the leading tone can rise. Example [recon]: bass G2 → A2, soprano D5 → C5, alto B4 → C5.
  - **Phrygian HC [recon], A minor iv6–V:** bass F3 → E3 while the soprano moves D5 → E5, so the outer voices expand from a 6th to an octave.
- **(c) Weight.** High. PAC/HC/IAC are ubiquitous and essential to phrase form, and they connect to Caplin's formal functions.
- **(d) Breaks.**
  - **Phrygian cadence between movements.** Bach's Brandenburg Concerto no. 3, the 2nd "movement" is just this cadence (E minor).
  - **Deceptive cadences as phrase extension (Classical).** Wagner's *Tristan* prelude delays an awaited tonic with a deceptive arrival on F (VI of A minor, around m. 17). This creates "endless melody" and avoids closure.
  - **"Evaded" cadences (Schmalfeldt/Caplin).** The expected tonic is replaced and the cadence is restarted ("one more time").
  - **Classical concerto.** The cadential 6/4 is expanded into a whole cadenza.

## 3. Seventh chords and non-chord tones

### Takeaway
- **Seventh chords.** Treatises stress V7 above all; Kostka gives it its own chapter. ii7/ii6/5 and vii°7/viiø7 come next, other diatonic sevenths last. The strict rule: in strict Baroque style the 7th is prepared, being held over from the previous chord as a common tone, and the 7th always resolves down by step. The Classical period relaxed preparation for V7.
- **Non-chord tones** get two chapters in Kostka. Suspensions are the most rule-bound; passing and neighbor tones are the most common.

### Cited Findings
- Kostka-Payne Part III covers sevenths in three chapters: "The V7 Chord", "The II7 and VII7 Chords", "Other Diatonic Seventh Chords". Non-chord tones get two chapters ("Nonchord Tones 1/2"). — [McGraw-Hill TOC](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0078025141/information_center_view0/table_of_contents.html)
- In Fenaroli's rule of the octave, degrees 4 and 7 ascending take 6/5/3 (= ii6/5 and V6/5) and degree 4 descending takes 4/2 (= V4/2). So seventh chords are integral to the partimento scale harmonization. — [Gettysburg](https://musictheory.sites.gettysburg.edu/?p=1533)
- Aldwell-Schachter has dedicated chapters on Leading-Tone Seventh Chords and on Melodic and Rhythmic Figuration. — [ECU catalog](https://librarycatalog.ecu.edu/catalog/1752420)
- OMT2e treats "Altered and Extended Chords" as a single section. It distinguishes "fake" extensions (non-chord tones) from "real" ones and notes the rarity of 11ths. — [LibreTexts OMT2e](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/05%3A_Chromaticism)

### Inferences

**V7 [std]**
- **(b) Rules.**
  - The 7th (4) goes down to 3; the leading tone goes up to 1.
  - Root-position V7–I: one of the two chords is incomplete. Either an incomplete V7 (no 5th, root doubled) goes to a complete I, or a complete V7 goes to an incomplete I (tripled root, no 5th), because the leading tone resolves up and the 7th down.
- **(b) Example [recon], C major, V7–I.**

  | Voice | V7 | I |
  |---|---|---|
  | S | F5 | E5 |
  | A | D5 | C5 |
  | T | B4 | C5 |
  | B | G2 | C3 |

  - The V7 is complete; the I is incomplete with a tripled root.
- **(d) Exception taught in textbooks.** In V4/3–I6 the 7th may rise, moving in parallel 10ths with the bass. Example [recon]: soprano F4 → G4 over bass D3 → E3.
- **(d) Breaks.**
  - Unresolved, "liberated" dominant sevenths in Debussy, and in Satie (major sevenths).
  - Parallel dominant sevenths sliding chromatically, e.g. Chopin's Prelude in E minor op. 28 no. 4, with slowly descending inner voices.
  - In late-19th-century music, the 7th is transferred to another voice or left hanging.

**ii7/ii6/5, vii°7, viiø7 [std]**
- **ii6/5.** The 7th (1) is prepared and held, then descends to 7 in V. Example [recon], C major ii6/5–V: bass F3 → G3; the chord's 7th C5 → B4.
- **vii°7 (minor and borrowed in major).** The 7th (♭6) goes down to 5; the tritones resolve inward/outward. Unequal 5ths need care: textbooks commonly allow d5 → P5 in the upper voices but not between the bass and an upper voice [std].
- **viiø7.** Typically viiø7 → V(6/5) or → I with doubled 3rd.
- **Other diatonic sevenths** (IV7, vi7, iii7, I7). These appear mainly in descending-5ths sequences. Weight: low except in sequences.

**Non-chord tones [std]**
- **Passing tone.** Stepwise between two chord tones, weak beat. Example [recon]: soprano E5 D5 C5 over sustained I.
- **Neighbor tone.** Step away and back: E5 F5 E5.
- **Suspension (strict):** preparation (consonant, held), suspension (dissonant, strong beat), resolution (down by step).
  - Types: 4–3, 7–6, 9–8, and in the bass 2–3.
  - Example 4–3 [recon]: soprano C5 tied over to V, where it becomes a 4th above bass G3, then resolves C5 → B4.
  - Do not double the note of resolution against a suspension, except in 9–8 with the bass.
- **Appoggiatura.** Approached by leap, accented, resolved by step.
- **Escape tone (échappée).** Step out, leap back in the opposite direction.
- **Anticipation.** Sounds a note of the next chord early; typical at cadences: soprano C5 (anticipating) C5.
- **Pedal point.** See §6.
- **(c) Weight.** High. Present in nearly every bar of the repertoire, and they make chorale textures idiomatic.
- **(d) Breaks.**
  - **Corelli's chains of 7–6 suspensions** over stepwise bass descents. This extends the rule rather than breaking it.
  - **The *Tristan* G#4 → A4 appoggiatura over the Tristan chord.** The non-chord tone becomes so long and accented that the "real" chord is ambiguous; this ambiguity is the basis of chromatic tonality.
  - **Unresolved appoggiaturas and "added-note" chords** (added 6th, added 9th) in Debussy and Ravel, where they are absorbed as chord tones.
  - **Romantic "sigh" appoggiaturas (Mozart, Chopin)** carry the expressive weight.

## 4. Sequences

### Takeaway
Sequences rank medium-high. In Kostka they are paired with harmonic progression ("Harmonic Progression and the Sequence"); OMT2e adds chromatic and parallel-chromatic sequences. Partimento pedagogy teaches them as bass *moti* and named schemata (Monte, Fonte, Romanesca, etc.). Strict rule: inside a diatonic sequence, chords like vii° in root position and doubled leading tones are tolerated because the sequential pattern overrides local rules. The pattern must be exact in every voice.

### Cited Findings
- **Kostka-Payne chapter 7** is titled "Harmonic Progression and the Sequence", making sequences part of the core diatonic unit. — [McGraw-Hill TOC](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0078025141/information_center_view0/table_of_contents.html)
- **OMT2e chromatic sequences sections:**
  - "Chromatic Sequences"
  - "Parallel Chromatic Sequences": parallel 6/3 chords, parallel dominants, parallel augmented triads
  - "Equal Divisions of the Octave": "nineteenth-century use of symmetrical divisions... to blur tonal centers"
  - Source: [LibreTexts OMT2e](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/05%3A_Chromaticism)

### Inferences

Pitch-name examples below are all [recon], in C major.

**Descending 5ths (circle of 5ths) — the most common**
- Roots: I–IV–vii°–iii–vi–ii–V–I.
- Bass [recon]: C3 F2 B2 E2 A2 D3 G2 C3.
- Variants:
  - alternating root position and first inversion: I – IV6 – vii° – iii6 …
  - all sevenths: 7–7 interlocking, with 7ths prepared and resolved in chain
  - with secondary dominants: V7/vi – V7/ii …
- Repertoire: ubiquitous in Vivaldi, Bach and Handel. Weight: highest of the sequences.

**Ascending 5–6** (ascending by step, rising 4th / falling 3rd)
- Bass C3 C3 D3 D3 E3 E3; upper voice G4 A4 A4 B4 B4 C5. Resulting harmonies: I – vi6 – ii – vii°6 – iii …
- The 5–6 motion breaks the parallel 5ths that a plain ascending root-position stepwise progression would create.

**Descending 5–6 / "Pachelbel" / descending-3rds** (down 4th, up 2nd)
- Roots I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V. The Pachelbel Canon is the archetype.

**Descending stepwise 6/3 chains** (fauxbourdon)
- Parallel first-inversion chords with 8–7 or 7–6 motion: bass E3 D3 C3 B2 A2 under soprano C5 B4 A4 G4 F4.

**Ascending 5ths**
- Rare; mostly in Romantic music.

**Partimento/galant schemata (Riepel, Gjerdingen)**
- **Monte:** ascending-step sequence, each step tonicized: V7/IV – IV, V7/V – V.
- **Fonte:** descending-step: V7/ii – ii, V7/I – I.
- **Ponte:** a dominant pedal.

**(d) Breaks and extensions**
- **Chromatic descending sequences** in parallel diminished sevenths or dominant sevenths; Chopin and Liszt.
- **Sequences by major 3rd (equal division of the octave).** Example: Liszt; the Schubert "Great" C major symphony has sequences by 3rds (from memory). Effect: tonal center blurred, symmetrical "non-diatonic" motion.
- **Wagner's *Tristan* prelude.** The opening phrase is sequenced up by minor 3rds: A minor → C → E (from memory). The sequence becomes the motivic and harmonic engine.
- **"Real" (transposed exactly) vs "tonal" (diatonic) sequences.** Romantic music favors real sequences that leave the key.

### Gaps
- **Partimento sources not fetched.** Gjerdingen's *Music in the Galant Style* and specific Fenaroli moti pages were not fetched. The Monte/Fonte descriptions are [std] from Gjerdingen, attributed to Riepel (1755).
- **No frequency data** on sequence types was found.

## 5. Chromatic harmony I: secondary dominants, applied leading-tone chords, mixture, Neapolitan, augmented 6ths, ninths and other "minor" topics

### Takeaway
Secondary (applied) dominants and leading-tone chords are the most important chromatic topic: two chapters in Kostka, and chapters in Puget Sound and Aldwell-Schachter. Mixture, the Neapolitan and the augmented sixths form a "chromatic predominant" cluster, together worth about 2 chapters. The dominant ninth, the augmented triad and 11th/13th chords are minor topics: sections only, with OMT2e explicitly calling the augmented triad "rare" and 11ths a "rarity".

### Cited Findings
- **Kostka-Payne:** "Secondary Functions 1" and "2" (2 chapters); "Mode Mixture and the Neapolitan" (1 shared chapter); "Augmented Sixth Chords" (1). Ninths, CT°7 and similar topics are grouped in "Further Elements of the Harmonic Vocabulary" (1). — [McGraw-Hill TOC](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0078025141/information_center_view0/table_of_contents.html)
- **Puget Sound** separates "Secondary Dominant Chords" and "Secondary Diminished Chords", with separate chapters for Mode Mixture, the Neapolitan, and Augmented Sixth Chords. — [Puget Sound TOC](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/frontmatter.html)
- **Augmented 6th spellings (scale degrees, minor-mode basis):**
  - It+6: ♭6, ♯4, 1
  - Fr+6: those plus 2
  - Ger+6: those plus ♭3
  - "EnGer+6" respells ♭3 as ♯2 and "resolves only to major I6/4"
  - Source: [Puget Sound, Types of Augmented Sixth Chords](https://musictheory.pugetsound.edu/mt21c/TypesOfAugmentedSixthChords.html)
- **OMT2e definitions:**
  - Mixture borrows notes from the parallel key to alter chord quality while keeping function, e.g. the Picardy third.
  - The Neapolitan (♭II6) is a chromatic predominant, usually in first inversion, resolving to V.
  - Augmented sixths "lack a root and resolve to dominant harmony".
  - The augmented triad is "rare", often III+ in harmonic minor or a link between V and I.
  - Source: [LibreTexts OMT2e](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/05%3A_Chromaticism)

### Inferences

**Secondary dominants and leading-tone chords [std]**
- **(a) What it is.** V(7)/x and vii°(7)/x of any major or minor diatonic triad except vii°, which cannot be tonicized.
- **(b) Rules.**
  - The temporary leading tone rises; the temporary 7th falls.
  - Do not double the chromatic leading tone.
  - Avoid a cross-relation, e.g. F in one voice followed by F# in another.
- **(b) Example [recon], C major, V6/5/V – V.**

  | Voice | V6/5/V | V |
  |---|---|---|
  | S | C5 | B4 |
  | A | A4 | G4 |
  | T | D4 | D4 |
  | B | F#3 | G3 |

- **(c) Weight.** Highest among chromatic topics, and extremely common: V/V and V/ii in Classical music, V/IV in Baroque.
- **(d) Extensions.**
  - Chains of secondary dominants (V/V/V… e.g. E7–A7–D7–G7–C) and back-relating dominants.
  - Romantic extended tonicization merges into modulation.
  - In Wagner and Franck, applied dominants resolve deceptively or to other applied chords, so tonics are perpetually postponed.

**Mode mixture [std]**
- **(a) Chords.** In major, from the parallel minor: iv, ii°, iiø7, ♭VI, ♭III, ♭VII, vii°7, and minor v (rare). In minor, the Picardy-third I.
- **(b) Rules.** Mixture keeps the chord's function. Spell ♭6 resolving down to 5 (A♭ → G in C).
- **(b) Example [recon], C major, IV–iv–I over a C3 tonic pedal:** inner voice A4 → A♭4 → G4 (♭6 falling to 5).
- **(c) Weight.** Medium; very common in Romantic music.
- **(d) Extensions.**
  - Brahms' Symphony no. 3 motto F–A♭–F, a major/minor ambiguity that pervades the work.
  - Schubert's major/minor alternations, e.g. *Winterreise*: the lied "Gute Nacht" turns to major in the final strophe (from memory).
  - ♭VI as a key area (Beethoven, Schubert), so mixture becomes large-scale tonal planning.

**Neapolitan sixth [std + cited]**
- **(a) What it is.** ♭II6, a predominant.
- **(b) Rules.**
  - Double the bass (4).
  - ♭2 moves down to 7, often through the cadential 6/4: ♭2–1–7 (D♭–C–B in C).
  - Avoid the diminished-3rd leap ♭2 → ♯7 unless filled in.
- **(b) Example [recon], C minor, N6 – V7 – i.**

  | Voice | N6 | V7 | i |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | S | D♭5 | B4 | C5 |
  | A | A♭4 | F4 | E♭4 |
  | T | F4 | D4 | C4 (G3 also possible) |
  | B | F3 | G3 | C3 |

- **(c) Weight.** Low-medium; usually half a chapter.
- **(d) Breaks.**
  - Beethoven's "Moonlight" Sonata op. 27 no. 2, I, m. 3 has a D-major N6 in C# minor (from memory). This is the textbook example.
  - Root-position ♭II, and ♭II tonicized as a key area: Beethoven's Appassionata op. 57 opening phrase is repeated a half-step higher on G♭ (from memory). Schubert, Chopin.
  - In Romantic music, ♭II becomes a tonal region (Schoenberg's "Neapolitan region").

**Augmented sixths [cited + std]**
- **Spellings in C minor:**
  - It+6: A♭–C–F#
  - Fr+6: A♭–C–D–F#
  - Ger+6: A♭–C–E♭–F#
- **(b) Rules.**
  - The A6 interval (♭6–♯4) expands outward to the octave on 5.
  - Do not double ♯4 or ♭6.
  - The Ger+6 normally goes to the cadential 6/4 first, which avoids parallel 5ths (E♭ → D).
  - Otherwise you get "Mozart fifths" (Ger+6 → V), tolerated mainly in Classical and Romantic music, especially with inner voices.
- **(b) Example [recon], C minor, Ger+6 – i6/4 – V – i.**

  | Voice | Ger+6 | i6/4 | V |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | B | A♭2 | G2 | G2 |
  | S | F#4 | G4 | G4 |
  | A | E♭4 | E♭4 | D4 |
  | T | C4 | C4 | B3 |

- **(c) Weight.** Medium; one chapter everywhere. Very common in Classical and Romantic cadential preparations.
- **(d) Extensions.**
  - Enharmonic Ger+6 = V7 of ♭II, a pivot for modulation by half step.
  - The common-tone (CT) +6 embellishing I (OMT2e); Schubert, Tchaikovsky.
  - The Fr+6, a whole-tone sonority, becomes a color chord in Debussy and Russian music.
  - The *Tristan* chord can be read as a Fr+6-like sonority with appoggiatura (several standard analyses).

**Minor topics** (sections, not chapters; my recommendation is ≤1 lesson each)
- **Dominant ninth (V9)**
  - Rule [std]: the 9th resolves down, usually with the 5th omitted in four parts. The 9th sits above the leading tone; the ninth must be at least a 9th above the root, not a 2nd.
  - Rimsky-Korsakov and Dubois give it a section. Low frequency in common practice; much more common in Romantic and Impressionist music and in jazz.
  - Break: Debussy's parallel ninth chords and Ravel; jazz extensions treat 9/11/13 as stable.
- **vii°7 used as V9 without its root** (the French/German tradition).
  - Rameau and some 19th-century treatises explain vii°7 this way [std]. Theory-history point, minor.
- **Augmented triad** (III+, V+): "rare" per OMT2e.
- **11th/13th chords:** rarity of 11ths per OMT2e; 13ths appear as V7 with an appoggiatura 6.
- **Altered dominants** (V+, V7♭5): minor.

### Gaps
- **Unverified repertoire.** The Beethoven, Brahms and Schubert examples are from memory. Before the app cites measure numbers, they should be verified against scores (IMSLP).
- **Mozart fifths.** No source was fetched for the "Mozart fifths" term itself. It is widely used in Aldwell-Schachter and Kostka [std].

## 6. Chromatic voice leading: chromatic mediants, common-tone diminished sevenths, the omnibus, linear chromaticism, and late-19th-century practice

### Takeaway
These are late-curriculum topics. Kostka covers them in one "Further Elements" chapter and one "Late 19th Century" chapter, while OMT2e spreads them over about 8 sections. Strict common practice had little to say. They describe how 19th-century composers extended voice leading: smooth semitone motion and common tones replace root-progression logic.

### Cited Findings
- **OMT2e common-tone chords:** CT°7 and CT+6 are "embellishing chords built from neighbor tones and chromatic tones, sharing a common tone with I or V". — [LibreTexts OMT2e](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/05%3A_Chromaticism)
- **OMT2e other sections:**
  - The omnibus is "a chromatic sequence with contrary bass motion, used to prolong dominant harmony or reach distant keys".
  - Mediants are classified as diatonic, chromatic, and doubly chromatic by number of common tones.
  - Neo-Riemannian P/L/R transformations and the "cube dance".
  - Equal division of the octave blurs tonal centers.
  - Source: [LibreTexts OMT2e](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/05%3A_Chromaticism)
- **Omnibus in Schubert:** the basic omnibus occurs in Schubert's Piano Sonata in A minor D. 845 (transition from first to second theme). In its full form the bass descends an entire octave chromatically. — [Wikipedia, Omnibus progression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omnibus_progression) (secondary; verify)
- **Chromatic mediant in Schubert:** in the Menuetto of Schubert's String Quartet no. 13 (D. 804), the recapitulation turns to C# minor, with C as a common tone across mediant-related tonic triads. Common-tone mediant relations are a central theme of Kopp's *Chromatic Transformations in Nineteenth-Century Music* (Cambridge, 2002). — [BU open access, Schubert DFT/Tonnetz analysis](https://open.bu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/2fcdfd8a-4706-4316-95fb-7b97ef32a290/content); [Cambridge, Kopp](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/chromatic-transformations-in-nineteenthcentury-music/chromatic-mediant-relations-in-musical-contexts/7C6FCA05A9478861AFC4F621F19A0D4B)
- **CT°7 and CT+6 as voice-leading events:** the MTO review of Kopp treats them as closely related. — [MTO 10.1, Bass review of Kopp](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.04.10.1/mto.04.10.1.bass.html)
- **Schubert CT°7 example:** a Schubert passage moves from A♭ major to a B diminished seventh via the common tone A♭. — [teoria.com article](https://www.teoria.com/en/articles/2022/7d/)

### Inferences

**Common-tone diminished seventh [std]**
- **(a) What it is.** A diminished seventh sharing one tone with the chord it embellishes. In C major: D#–F#–A–C around C–E–G, i.e. I – CT°7 – I.
- **(b) Example [recon], four parts, I – CT°7 – I:**
  - S E5 D#5 E5
  - A C5 C5 C5 (the common tone)
  - T G4 A4 G4 (A4 is an upper neighbor)
  - B C3 C3 C3
  - That gives D#–A–C over C. For the full °7 (D#–F#–A–C), use five voices or replace the doubled C with F#4 (from G4).
  - Spell the chord by its resolution: raised lower neighbors.
- **(c) Weight.** Low-medium; common in Romantic waltzes and lieder.
- **What it accomplishes.** Chromatic color without a change of function.

**Chromatic mediants**
- **(a) What it is.** Roots a 3rd apart, of the same quality, with one common tone: C major → E major / A♭ major / A major / E♭ major.
- **(c) Weight.** Low in strict treatises, which barely treat it; high in Romantic repertoire.
- **(d) Repertoire (from memory, verify).**
  - Beethoven's "Waldstein" op. 53 exposition, C major → E major second theme.
  - Schubert's Sonata D. 960 opening, B♭ → G♭.
  - Liszt and Wagner (Neo-Riemannian analyses).
- **What it accomplishes.** Bright or dark "shifts" without dominant preparation; key relations by 3rds replace fifth-relations as a form-shaping force.

**Omnibus** (cited above)
- **(b) Example [recon], prolonging V in C.**
  - Outer voices move chromatically in contrary motion: the bass descends G3 F#3 F3 E3… while the soprano ascends B4 C5 C#5 D5.
  - Chords alternate V7 – Ger+6/°7 – V6/4 … (exact spellings vary by source).
- **Weight.** Low.

**Linear chromaticism / "vagrant chords" (Schoenberg)**
- Diminished sevenths and augmented triads have no fixed key and serve as pivots.
- The *Tristan* opening, Chopin's Prelude op. 28 no. 4 and the late Liszt are the standard repertoire for this.
- Function becomes a product of semitone voice leading.

**Late-19th-century summary (Kostka ch. 25, [std])**
- Counterpoint-generated harmony.
- Unresolved dissonance.
- Tonal ambiguity, including pieces that begin off-tonic.
- Directional tonality: pieces ending in a key other than their opening key (Mahler).

- **Schoenberg's own text unavailable.** *Harmonielehre* chapters on "vagrant chords" (*vagierende Akkorde*) and *Structural Functions* region charts were not accessible online this session.

## 7. Pedal points, harmonic rhythm, textural realization (and a brief note on modulation)

### Takeaway
None of these get their own chapter in Kostka:
- Pedal points sit inside the non-chord-tone and 6/4 chapters.
- Harmonic rhythm sits in the progression and phrase chapters.
- Texture appears in the figuration part of Aldwell-Schachter and in partimento realization.

They matter hugely in practice, so in an app they work best as recurring "lenses" rather than big chapters. Modulation (2–3 chapters in Kostka) is covered by another researcher.

### Cited Findings
- **Aldwell-Schachter** devotes a part to "Elements of figuration" and has a chapter on "Melodic and Rhythmic Figuration". — [ECU catalog](https://librarycatalog.ecu.edu/catalog/1752420)
- **Kostka-Payne modulation chapters:** "Modulations Using Diatonic Common Chords", "Some Other Modulatory Techniques", and "Enharmonic Spellings and Enharmonic Modulations". — [McGraw-Hill TOC](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0078025141/information_center_view0/table_of_contents.html)
- **OMT2e chromatic modulation:** pivot chords, common-tone modulation, enharmonic reinterpretation, and reinterpreting diminished sevenths (each note can be a root). — [LibreTexts OMT2e](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Music/Music_Theory/Open_Music_Theory_2e_(Gotham_et_al.)/05%3A_Chromaticism)
- **Partimento:** the bass is the given, and the student realizes the upper voices (texture) at the keyboard from the bass. — [Wikipedia, Partimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partimento)

### Inferences

**Pedal point [std]**
- **(b) Rules.**
  - A sustained tone, usually 1 or 5 and usually in the bass.
  - It begins and ends as a chord tone; the harmonies above may be dissonant with it.
- **(b) Example [recon], C major.** Bass C3 held under I – IV6/4 – vii°7/… – V7 – I.
- **(d) Repertoire (from memory).**
  - Bach's WTC I Prelude in C, closing tonic pedal; the Bach organ toccatas use dominant pedals before the recapitulation of the theme.
  - Wagner's *Das Rheingold* prelude: an E♭ major sonority sustained about 136 bars. The pedal becomes the whole form, depicting a static world-origin.
  - Classical sonata retransitions: long dominant pedals (Gjerdingen's "Ponte"). These create expectation.

**Harmonic rhythm [std / Caplin]**
- **Rules.**
  - Chord changes are usually regular.
  - Harmonic rhythm accelerates toward cadences (Caplin's sentence: the continuation fragments and speeds up).
  - A chord should change across a bar line rather than hold from a weak beat to a strong beat; avoid "syncopated" harmony in strict exercises.
  - The cadential 6/4 goes on a strong beat.
- **Breaks.** Static harmony for long spans: Wagner, Debussy, Bruckner.

**Textural realization [std]**
- Alberti bass, arpeggiation (*Brechung*), compound melody, keyboard style (three upper voices in the right hand) vs chorale style.
- In Kostka-Payne's terms, these are surface textures over an underlying four-voice framework.
- **Why it matters for an app.** The app should teach students to reduce figuration to its chorale skeleton and to elaborate a skeleton into figuration.

**Modulation (brief).** Common-chord (pivot), chromatic, common-tone, enharmonic (Ger+6 ↔ V7, °7 respelling), and sequential modulation. Closely related keys dominate Classical practice; third-related and distant keys dominate Romantic practice.

- **Caplin not fetched.** Caplin's *Classical Form* was not fetched. The acceleration-to-cadence claim is [std].
